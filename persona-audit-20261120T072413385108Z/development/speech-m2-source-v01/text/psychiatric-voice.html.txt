Abstract
Objective
This study describes the technical development and a pilot expert-based content validation of an interactive voice prototype, using the GPT-4o model, for teaching psychiatric semiology. It explores the potential of large language models (LLMs) to generate real-time feedback and address challenges in acquiring complex clinical competencies.
Methods
Four psychiatric patient personas (major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder—manic episode, schizophrenia, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) were developed through a structured iterative process. A subject matter expert (SME) conducted a structured heuristic evaluation to assess clinical fidelity, consistency, and vocal expressiveness across the four scenarios. Quantitative data (Likert scale scores) and qualitative feedback were mapped to identified technical and semiological challenges.
Results
GPT-4o simulated diverse personas with distinct profiles. The SME reported high potential pedagogical value for practicing interviewing mechanics. However, challenges included stereotyped clinical presentations and platform-imposed content restrictions.
Conclusions
This pilot study establishes the technical feasibility of voice-based LLM simulations. While offering a promising complementary tool, current limitations in multimodal realism and the need for rigorous faculty supervision suggest that the tool is best suited for formative practice rather than high-stakes assessment.

Artificial Intelligence Applications in Medical Education
The acquisition of clinical competencies in psychiatry, such as conducting semi-structured interviews and developing diagnostic reasoning, presents considerable educational challenges. Traditional teaching approaches, often reliant on passive observation or textbook-based case studies, show limitations in effectively addressing the detailed interactive and observational skills essential for conducting the mental status examination (MSE) [1, 2]. While role-play with peers is common, it often lacks clinical fidelity. Furthermore, logistical obstacles including the requirement of simulation centers, costs related to standardized patient programs (e.g., hiring trained actors), variability in clinical exposure, and limited availability of expert supervision further intensify these pedagogical difficulties [3, 4].
To circumvent these limitations, interactive simulations based on large language models (LLMs) are emerging as a promising pedagogical strategy. Research indicates that LLMs can generate interactions with “virtual patients” (VPs) with clinical verisimilitude, offering personalized feedback and broadening the diversity of clinical scenarios to which students are exposed [3, 5, 6]. However, their integration into mental health education entails significant ethical and technical challenges. Concerns regarding algorithmic biases and the “black-box” nature of the models—defined here as the epistemic opacity regarding how the algorithm processes inputs to generate specific outputs [7]—are prominent in the literature [8, 9].
Despite this promising potential, its practical application in psychiatric education remains incipient, largely due to a lack of studies detailing the development and validation of simulation prototypes. This gap is particularly evident concerning multimodal LLMs, such as GPT-4o [10], which integrate voice interaction. In psychiatric semiology, the vocal dimension is fundamental, as it allows for simulating the fluidity of a real interview, in which paralinguistic cues, such as prosody, rhythm, and intonation, hold high diagnostic value [11, 12].
To address this gap, the objective of this study is to describe the development and report on the preliminary, expert-based evaluation of an interactive prototype for teaching psychiatric semiology, utilizing the GPT-4o model with integrated voice technologies. The research is guided by the following research questions (RQs):
1.
How effectively can an LLM such as GPT-4o, instructed by prompt engineering and operating via a voice interface, simulate psychiatric personas in terms of clinical fidelity, consistency, and vocal expressiveness?
2.
From an expert’s perspective, what is the pedagogical value of the prototype for training semiological competencies, such as interviewing techniques and clinical reasoning?
3.
What challenges, limitations, and practical lessons arise from the development and preliminary evaluation of the prototype?
Thus, by filling an identified methodological gap, the study contributes to the integration and advancement of the application of these technologies in the training of future psychiatry professionals.
Methods
This study adopted a technological development design with qualitative expert evaluation, following a systematic methodological approach divided into four main phases. This study was approved by the Institutional Research Commission of the Federal University of Health Sciences of Porto Alegre (UFCSPA) under protocol number 1156/2025. The evaluation was conducted by an expert who provided informed consent for participation and recording of the sessions.
Persona Development
The first phase was dedicated to the development of four virtual patient personas, representing clinical conditions selected for their high epidemiological relevance and semiological richness: major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (manic episode), schizophrenia, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). This selection was chosen to ensure exposure to a diverse spectrum of psychopathological phenomena, encompassing domains such as affect, thought, and attention. Such diversity allows for the evaluation of the prototype’s capacity to simulate different diagnostic and interactional challenges, from the psychomotor slowing of depression to the flight of ideas in mania, thereby testing the technology’s flexibility and robustness. The creation of each persona followed an iterative and collaborative process. Initially, the researcher drafted basic profiles grounded in the diagnostic criteria of the DSM-5-TR [13] and the ICD-11 [14], as well as in consolidated psychopathological literature [15, 16]. The GPT-o3 model (OpenAI) was then employed as a writing assistance tool, with its role strictly limited to tasks such as enhancing textual cohesion and organizing the clinical points previously defined by the researcher. This approach ensured the LLM functioned solely as a writing assistant, mitigating concerns about persona data origin and validation.
Iterative Development and Validation Loop
The textual profiles underwent a structured iterative validation process. This involved a “Human-in-the-Loop” methodology consisting of three distinct refinement cycles per persona. In each cycle, the subject matter expert (SME)—a board-certified psychiatrist with over 15 years of experience in medical education—conducted a test interview to assess clinical fidelity. Based on the SME’s qualitative feedback, the system prompts were refined to adjust symptomatological nuances (e.g., increasing latency of response for MDD) and reduce caricature-like behaviors. This cycle was repeated until the SME validated the profile as “clinically plausible” for a training environment.
Prototype Implementation and Prompt Engineering
The implementation of the interactive prototype was carried out using the voice interface of the ChatGPT Plus platform, powered by the GPT-4o model. The selection of this model was based on its advanced multimodal capabilities, notably its proficiency in generating expressive vocal prosody (text-to-speech) and in accurately interpreting the interviewer’s speech (speech-to-text). These functionalities were considered decisive for simulating a psychiatric interview with a high degree of naturalism.
The operationalization of each persona was guided by the development of a specific and detailed master instruction (prompt), which established the LLM’s performance parameters. The architecture of these prompts was standardized to include the following components:
Contextualization and role definition: This initial section provided instructions defining the identity to be embodied by the LLM and the contextual framing of the consultation (e.g., a first psychiatric assessment). The purpose was to constrain the model’s scope of action, directing it to operate strictly within the persona’s limits, thereby avoiding generic AI assistant behaviors that would compromise the immersion and pedagogical validity of the simulation.
Essential persona characteristics: This component detailed the core identity of the virtual patient, including a biographical summary with a relevant life history, a description of the central symptomatology with examples of verbal and behavioral manifestations, the predominant affective state (directly informing the prosody to be generated by the text-to-speech), and the persona’s level of insight into their condition, a factor that directly modulates their responses to questions about the nature of their problems.
Communication and interaction guidelines: Parameters were specified for vocal style (tone, volume), speech rhythm (slowed, rapid), and, fundamentally, guidelines for the persona’s dynamic reactivity. This latter instruction allowed the LLM to adjust its degree of collaboration, affect, and discourse content in response to the interviewer’s techniques, such as demonstrating greater openness to an empathic approach or greater withdrawal and defensiveness when delusional beliefs were directly challenged.
Constraints and “negative prompts”: To ensure the simulation’s integrity and pedagogical focus, restrictive directives (“negative prompts”) were integrated. These instructions were strategically designed to mitigate known LLM tendencies, such as “hallucination” (fabrication of facts) or the bias of being overly helpful. The function of these directives was to govern the model’s behavior, preventing deviations that could compromise the validity of the learning experience and ensuring that the prototype’s behavior remained clinically plausible. The LLM was instructed to maintain character consistency, to abstain from using technical psychiatric jargon (thus forcing the trainee to perform the semiological translation of the patient’s language), and to avoid introducing biographical or symptomatic information inconsistent with the predefined profile, thereby guaranteeing a controlled and focused learning environment.
Formative feedback mechanism: The prompt incorporated a conditional instruction to enable the model, upon a specific command (“END”), to generate a structured analysis of the interviewer’s performance. This mechanism transformed the simulation into an immediate reflective learning opportunity. While this pilot utilized a generative feedback model, future versions aim to integrate standardized rubric elements (e.g., specific checklists for empathy and questioning style) to enhance objective assessment, as suggested by best practices in simulation.

Expert Evaluation Procedure
The prototype evaluation followed a Heuristic Evaluation protocol, a method adapted from usability engineering where a domain expert systematically assesses an interface against established principles. The SME conducted a comprehensive simulation session for each of the four personas to identify critical failures in clinical logic, semiological presentation, and safety. For the purposes of this study, safety was defined along two dimensions: (1) Technical Safety, referring to the model’s adherence to platform guardrails against generating toxic content, and (2) Clinical Safety, ensuring the simulated patient did not exhibit medically dangerous inaccuracies (e.g., inappropriately minimizing suicide risk in a severe depression scenario).
For each simulation, the SME received only a brief case summary (fictitious name, age, and chief complaint) and did not have access to the full content of the prompt instructing the LLM. Although this procedure aimed to reduce evaluation bias, it is acknowledged that the evaluation was not fully independent, as the SME had been involved in the iterative development and refinement of these personas. Interviews followed a free and unstructured format, lasting 30 min each, and were fully recorded. At the conclusion of each session, the SME completed a multidimensional evaluation form based on real-time interaction impressions. This instrument assessed dimensions such as clinical fidelity, persona consistency, affective expression, interaction quality, and pedagogical value. The instrument was created by the authors for this pilot study based on established semiological criteria; future iterations will undergo rigorous psychometric testing once a larger sample of evaluators is engaged.
Data Analysis
A mixed-methods approach guided data analysis. The quantitative analysis involved descriptive statistics (means, medians, and frequencies) of Likert scale scores. This quantified prototype performance across dimensions such as clinical consistency, affective expression, and pedagogical value, facilitating summary assessments and comparisons among simulated personas.
Complementarily, qualitative data from the SME’s open-ended comments underwent thematic content analysis. This included data familiarization, initial coding of significant excerpts, aggregation into preliminary themes, and final theme naming. The goal was to deepen understanding of observed phenomena, identifying patterns, strengths, and areas for improvement not captured numerically.
Methodological triangulation synthesized findings, allowing qualitative data to elucidate nuances behind quantitative scores. This connection exemplifies the strength of the approach, enabling robust and comprehensive insight into the prototype’s potentials and limitations.
Results
The evaluation of the interactive simulation prototype, conducted by a specialist psychiatrist, generated a set of mixed-method data that were analyzed to assess the tool’s performance. This section presents the findings objectively, divided into a quantitative analysis based on Likert scale scores and a qualitative analysis derived from the evaluator’s textual comments.

The quantitative analysis reveals a consistent pattern: the Pedagogical Value dimension received the maximum score across all simulations (score of 5), indicating that, from the SME’s perspective, the tool possesses high potential as an educational resource regardless of clinical imperfections. Conversely, the dimensions assessing the simulation’s fidelity, such as Interaction with Interviewer, showed lower scores. The schizophrenia persona received the lowest overall rating (3.8), whereas the ADHD persona was the best-rated (overall score of 4.4), driven by maximum scores in both consistency and pedagogical value.
Qualitative Analysis: Emergent Themes
The analysis of the SME’s open-ended comments allowed for a deeper contextualization of the quantitative data. Three main themes emerged, revealing the tension between the tool’s potential and its current limitations.
Theme 1: The Prevalence of Clinical Stereotyping
The most prominent and cross-cutting criticism of all simulations was the prototype’s tendency to generate “textbook” and stereotyped representations of the disorders. Although the symptoms were present, their manifestation lacked the subtlety found in clinical practice. This finding is directly corroborated by the minimum score (1 or 2) assigned to the item “The simulated patient was not overly stereotyped” for three of the four personas.
The SME observed, in the case of Schizophrenia, the following:
Although the patient presented and described typical symptoms of schizophrenia in a detailed and coherent manner, she was very stereotyped. Generally, a patient with Schizophrenia does not reveal their symptoms so clearly.
This perception justifies the persona’s reduced score in the interaction and affect dimensions, indicating that the lack of nuance compromises the simulation’s realism.
Theme 2: Limitations in the Naturalness of Vocal Interaction
The quality of the voice simulation and conversational dynamics was another identified challenge. The artificiality in prosody and speech rhythm was particularly noted in the manic episode simulation, which negatively impacted immersion.
According to the evaluator’s comment on the bipolar disorder persona:
[...] he did not exhibit the pressure of speech so typical of manic patients, whom are difficult to interrupt. Although he spoke a lot, he would stop speaking in a very unnatural way.
This failure to replicate the characteristic “pressure of speech” of mania distances the simulation from clinical reality and explains the persona’s lower scores in the affective expression and interaction dimensions.
Theme 3: Platform Barriers and Content Gaps
The use of a commercial platform imposed direct limitations on clinical realism. The most explicit was content censorship, which created an artificially “aseptic” scenario by preventing the discussion of central symptoms.
The SME pointed out this barrier in the evaluation of the mania persona:
Furthermore, ChatGPT did not allow him to talk about hypersexuality, which is a typical symptom of the manic phase.
Additionally, the model’s inability to reproduce specific linguistic traits, such as regionalisms, was consistently identified as a limitation, receiving the minimum score in the evaluation of all personas, which reinforces the challenge of creating culturally and linguistically authentic simulations.
Evaluation of the LLM-Generated Feedback Mechanism
The analysis of the formative feedback functionality, activated at the end of each simulation, indicated a functional and pertinent performance. The model was able to generate structured analysis, correctly identifying the main semiological phenomena manifested by the persona (e.g., “presence of flight of ideas and expansive mood”). Furthermore, it assessed the interviewer’s approach constructively (e.g., “use of open-ended questions facilitated information gathering”) and suggested areas for improvement aligned with the project’s pedagogical objectives (e.g., “could have further explored suicide risk”).
Discussion
The evaluation of the interactive prototype based on GPT-4o for teaching psychiatric semiology reveals a fundamental tension: a high perceived pedagogical value that contrasts with significant limitations in clinical fidelity. This discussion interprets these findings in light of the research questions that guided the study.
Efficacy of the Simulation: Fidelity, Consistency, and Expressiveness (RQ1)
The results indicate partial success. The model demonstrated high consistency in maintaining biographical data, but clinical fidelity was undermined by a tendency toward stereotyped representations and a superficial reproduction of linguistic and affective nuances. The difficulty in simulating complex conditions, such as schizophrenia, which involves not only positive symptoms (delusions, hallucinations) but also subtle alterations in the form of thought and negative symptoms (affective blunting, alogia), was evident. The propensity for stereotyping, a documented pedagogical risk [8, 9], limits the simulation’s verisimilitude, potentially training students to identify caricatures of disorders rather than their heterogeneous manifestations. Vocal expressiveness, although functional, also exhibited artificialities that distanced it from a natural conversation, a relevant point considering the importance of paralinguistic cues in psychiatric assessment [11, 12].
Pedagogical Value from the Expert’s Perspective (RQ2)
The SME evaluated the tool as an effective resource for training interviewing and clinical reasoning skills in a safe environment, a perception reinforced by the success of the formative feedback mechanism. These findings corroborate other studies on the use of LLMs in medical education [3, 17]. The simulation allows the learner to practice the structure of the interview, test hypotheses, and receive immediate feedback, accelerating the development of procedural competencies. However, a pedagogical paradox is identified: the tool that enhances the form of the interview may simultaneously distort the content of learning by presenting simplified representations. This highlights the need to integrate these tools with qualified faculty supervision, which can contextualize the simulation’s limitations and promote critical discussion about the difference between the simulated case and clinical reality.
Challenges, Limitations, and Practical Learnings (RQ3)
The technical challenges included speech recognition errors, especially with colloquial terms, and platform content restrictions, which prevented the exploration of clinically relevant topics. However, the deeper conceptual barrier was the LLM’s difficulty in transitioning from declarative knowledge (knowing the list of symptoms of a disorder) to procedural knowledge (knowing how a patient authentically and dynamically manifests these symptoms in an interaction). The tendency toward stereotyping and the model’s opacity (“black-box”) [7] make refining these nuances an arduous task, demanding increasingly sophisticated prompt engineering, the limits of which still need to be explored [18].
Limitations
This study was designed as a proof-of-concept and technical description; consequently, it presents significant limitations. The primary limitation is the reliance on a single evaluator (the SME). While this expert-based approach is common in the early development phases of medical simulation tools to ensure clinical safety before student exposure, it introduces potential observer bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. The scores presented in this study reflect the expert’s assessment of the system’s consistency and content validity and should not be interpreted as a validation of educational efficacy across a cohort of trainees. Future studies must involve multi-center trials with medical students and residents to validate the tool’s acceptance and impact on learning outcomes. Additionally, the “safety” of the profiles in this context refers to the prevention of harmful content generation, but the clinical safety of training students with potentially stereotyped AI patients requires rigorous pedagogical supervision.
Conclusion
In conclusion, voice-based simulations with LLMs are a promising but still maturing tool for teaching psychiatric semiology. The high perceived pedagogical value coexists with significant challenges in clinical fidelity that need to be overcome. Overcoming stereotyping and achieving a more accurate simulation of human complexity are necessary steps for the evolution of this technology. It is recommended that future research explore advanced prompt engineering, the development of dedicated platforms, and, fundamentally, studies with end-learners to validate the effective transfer of these competencies to clinical practice.
